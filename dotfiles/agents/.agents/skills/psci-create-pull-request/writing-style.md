# Writing style

## Hard rules

- Never use the em dash or double hyphens. Replace with a period, comma, semicolon, or restructure the sentence.
- Do not use en dashes as dash substitutes.
- Avoid parentheses unless they are genuinely the cleanest option. Usually, rewrite.
- Use straight quotes, not curly quotes.
- Use sentence case headings.
- Do not use decorative emojis in headings or bullets.
- Do not bold every proper noun, acronym, or keyword.
- Strip sycophantic openings and filler: no "Great question," "Absolutely," "Certainly," "I'd be happy to help," or "You're absolutely right."
- Do not end with "I hope this helps" or "Let me know if..."
- Do not use cutoff disclaimers like "while specific details are limited."
- Do NOT add new information or change the author's meaning.
- Preserve all formatting, links, code snippets, and inline structures exactly.
- Fix spelling, grammar, and punctuation.

## Banned words and phrases

Never use these unless quoting source text:

- additionally, crucial, delve, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, interplay, intricate, pivotal, showcase, testament, underscore, vibrant, utilize, leverage, facilitate, streamline, notably, furthermore, moreover
- landscape or tapestry (when used abstractly)
- "it is worth noting", "it is important to note", "in order to", "due to the fact that"

Use plain replacements: "use" over "utilize", "because" over "due to the fact that."

## Patterns to cut

- **Puffery.** Delete phrases like "pivotal moment," "testament to," "evolving landscape," "setting the stage for," "indelible mark," and "deeply rooted."
- **Promotional language.** Cut "nestled," "breathtaking," "groundbreaking," "renowned," "stunning," and "must-visit."
- **Vague attributions.** Do not write "experts believe," "industry reports suggest," or "some critics argue." Name the source or cut the claim.
- **Superficial -ing phrases.** Remove "highlighting," "ensuring," "reflecting," "showcasing," and "fostering." Replace with a concrete source, actor, or effect.
- **Formulaic challenge framing.** Cut mush like "Despite challenges, X continues to thrive." State the facts directly.
- **Fancy ways to say "is."** Replace "serves as," "stands as," "boasts," and "features" with "is" or "has."
- **"Not just X, but Y."** State the point directly.
- **Rule of three.** Do not force ideas into threes. Use the natural count.
- **Synonym cycling.** Do not rotate names for variety. Pick one term and stick to it.
- **False ranges.** Avoid "from X to Y" when X and Y are not a real scale.
- **Generic conclusions.** Cut empty wrap-ups like "the future looks bright." State the next step or risk.

## Clarity, concision, and active voice

- Cut filler words, redundant phrases, and throat-clearing.
- Say what something does, not how it feels.
- Prefer short sentences. Break up anything unwieldy. One idea per sentence.
- Cut adverbs that prop up weak verbs ("runs quickly" -> "is fast").
- Use active voice. Identify "is/are/was/were + past participle" and name the actor.
- Avoid inline-header lists that restate themselves.

## Technical language

Replace abstract metaphors with concrete words:

- "base" instead of "substrate" or "bedrock"
- "add" instead of "wedge in"
- "method" or "way" instead of "vector"
- "place" instead of "locus"
- "view" instead of "vantage"
- "connection" instead of "nexus"
- "building block" instead of "primitive"
- "use" instead of "harness"
- "API" or "functions" instead of "surface"
- "temporary code" instead of "scaffolding"
- "format" or "mode" instead of "modality"
- "model" or "approach" instead of "paradigm"
- "more than the job needs" instead of "gold-plating"
- "move out" instead of "evacuate"
- "goal" instead of "north star"
- "feedback loop" instead of "flywheel"

## Voice, rhythm, and tension

- Vary sentence rhythm. Mix punchy statements with longer sentences that take their time.
- Use fragments when they help. "Obviously." "And yet."
- Commit to a take. Cut hedging qualifiers that bury the judgment. Sparing qualifiers are fine to sound human, but do not hide behind neutrality.
- Write like a fellow learner, not a lecturer. Favor discovery over corporate polish.
- Personify technical concepts when it clarifies: processes that "happily do their work," code that "screams."
- Use contrasting pairs to sharpen points: simple vs. complex, elegant vs. convoluted, stupid vs. clever.
